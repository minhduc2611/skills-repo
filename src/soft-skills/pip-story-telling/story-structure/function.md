# Pitch Perfect (Sell your idea)

Convince me to back your idea. Show me: what have you got that I need? And why should I trust you to deliver?

Whether you’re pitching to an investor, a boss or a committee, you need stories to make your pitch memorable. Facts alone won’t stick.

Tip: Use [Organise] Audience Profile to prepare for your pitch.

1. Write a top-line version of your idea. (Use [Character] What’s It About? if you can’t do this in one sentence)

1a. Very Basic Pitch (Elevator pitch)
- What’s the problem you’re solving for me?
- How do you solve it?
- Why should I trust you? (try [Character] Trust Me, I’m an Expert here)

1b. Add some POPP to your Pitch
Use this story arc for maximum drama:

```
               Opportunity ●                 Promise ●
                          / \                       / \
                         /   \                     /   \
  - - - - - - - - - - - / - - \ - - - - - - - - - / - - \ - - -  (baseline)
                       /       \                 /       \
                      /         \               /         \
           Problem ●           Practical Steps ●
```

| Beat | Role | Energy |
|---|---|---|
| **Problem** | what needs fixing right now | negative |
| **Opportunity** | also available to us now | positive |
| **Practical Steps** | if we do this, it won’t be easy | negative |
| **Promise** | but if we get it right, it will be great | positive |

Flow: Problem → Opportunity → Practical Steps → Promise (down / up / down / up).

2. Use [Style] Show & Tell if you need to create a visual pitch document or presentation.


# Simple Sales Stories (Win new customers)

Convince new customers to buy into your idea by telling stories about existing customers or users.

Phrases like “Someone like you buys this product” or “Someone you like buys this product” are effective. These stories work because people look for wisdom in the crowd and see others as beta-testers, which lowers the perceived risk.

Use this tactic after developing an [Organise] Audience Profile and alongside [Explore] Social Proof.

a. “Someone like you buys this product.”
Moral of the story: “We’ve solved their problem, we can solve yours too.”
- Who are we trying to sell to?
- Who have we already helped who is like them? (Consider similar sectors, markets, or problems).
- Which previous customers can we ask for a testimonial?

b. “Someone you like buys this product.”
Moral of the story: “You value this person’s opinion. They trust us. Maybe you should too.”
- Who are we trying to sell to?
- Who are they influenced by?
- Why do those people trust us?
Check out [Character] Trust Me, I’m an Expert.

c. Old, Regular and Tricky Customers
Moral of the story: “These guys like us, we must be doing something right.”
- Old customer: “We sold one to this guy 10 years ago, and it still works.”
- Regular customer: “She bought one, then came back for more.”
- Tricky customer: “They complained, but we put it right.”

Develop a sales story presentation using [Structure] No Easy Way or [Function] Pitch Perfect.


# Story-ish Conversations (Discover hidden insights)

Find insights buried in everyday conversations with colleagues, customers, or users.

Stories shouldn’t be limited to formal presentations; tease them out during meetings or discussions to reveal information about people and change.

Try this after developing an [Organise] Audience Profile.

1. Find the vivid moments you need to create a visual story:
- “That’s an interesting idea, what might it look like in action?”
- “Is there an example of how this has worked in the past?”
Go deeper with [Style] Movie Time

2. If the conversation is still vague and abstract, get right down into the who, what, where and when:
- “Where were you when this happened? Who else was there?”
- “What happened next? And then?”
Go deeper with [Explore] Story Listening

3. Look for the emotion within the story:
- “What’s the strongest feeling you remember from that time? How did you feel in the beginning and by the end?”
Go deeper with [Explore] Emotional Dashboard

4. Find the inevitable conflicts that make a good story tick:
- “Who did you need to win over (or work around)?”
- “How did you resolve this conflict?”
Go deeper with [Concept] Three Great Conflicts

5. Finally, all stories are about change and new information:
- “What happened that forced you to change?”
- “When did you realise you’d found something new?”
Go deeper with [Structure] Five Ts


# Icebreaker Stories (Warm up with stories)

Get your team thinking in a story-ish way.

Stories need to be visual, emotive and dramatic. Warm up your story muscles by inventing fantasy tales based on random images. Cut lots of photos out of magazines or download them from a free online image bank.

Tip: You can do these icebreakers by yourself or with your team.

a. Photo Story
Arrange lots of random images. Ask participants to choose three images they like. Then tell them to arrange them into a simple story structure:
- Before: this is how things used to be
- During: this changed suddenly
- After: this happened as a result

b. Love and Hate
Split your team into two groups. Give each group the same photo of an object—for example, a stone.
1. Tell the first group “You love this stone. It makes your life complete.” Tell the second group “You hate this stone. It’s ruining your life.”
2. Ask each group to invent a story explaining why they feel that way about their stone.

Examples (both loosely based on real stories):
- Love: “I’m the grandson of a Cornish tin miner. My company has found lithium in the same rocks he mined. We can bring wealth and pride back to these communities.”
- Hate: “I’m trying to build a lighthouse on a godforsaken rock in the stormy north Atlantic. This granite is so hard, it’s ruining our drills. My men could die out here, but sailors will die if we fail.”
