# Movie Time (Help us see)

Tell me a story and a little movie starts playing in my head. I literally “see” what you mean.

The brain processes language by running a visual simulation. Abstract concepts (like “sustainability” or “accessibility”) are hard to visualise because nothing actually happens. Give us a specific moment to bring these abstracts to life.

If you’re struggling, try [Explore] Emotional Dashboard or [Concept] Good & Evil.

1. Start with the key concept or main point you want to get across. Find a movie moment that helps me “see” what you mean. (Use [Character] What’s It About? for help.)

a. ACTION!
- Where are we?
- What is happening?
- What happens next?

b. Emotion
- Who is involved?
- What is at stake?
- How does it feel?

2. Once you’ve set the scene with Action and Emotion, we’re ready to listen to the point you’d like to make.

c. Meaning
- What has changed, what have we learned?
- Why does this matter?
- What do you want us to think or do differently now?

Get into the habit of movie-checking your own writing. At the end of a paragraph, ask yourself “what’s the movie playing in their heads now?” If the answer is “none”, you’re not telling a story.

# Story Hooks (Grab our attention)

Craft a compelling hook at the start of your story. It will keep us listening til the end.

You might have the best story in the world, but it’s no use if you can’t get people to listen, to click on your link or open your email. You need a hook: something that makes us want to find out more. Shall I let you into the secret?

Tip: [Character] What’s It About? or [Concept] Curious Tales can help you find hooks in your story.

I looked at 1,000+ TED talks to see how their titles hook our attention. I spotted some themes: TED’s QUIRKS. Which could you use to hook us into your story?

a. Questions
What happens when you ask a question? We try to answer it in our heads, then we stick around to hear your answer.
Story Hook: can you start your story with a Big Question?

b. Unexpected
When everyone zigs, you zag. “Why you’ll fail” is an attention-grabbing take on a “How to succeed” story.
Story Hook: surprise your audience (but still make sense).

c. Ironies
If something ain’t what it should be, we want to know why. TED’s top talk “Schools kill creativity” reeks of irony.
Story Hook: tell us something is wrong.

d. Relatable
Writing about You, We and Us makes your reader feel more involved. “Is this you in this video?” is a guaranteed attention hook (and classic phishing con).
Story Hook: make it sound personal.

e. Knowledge
Your expertise, made simple.
Story Hook: rules, explainers, listicles, life-hacks and secrets all suggest expertise + insider status.

f. Superlatives
Biggest, fastest, newest all inspire wonder; while smallest, slowest, oldest provoke curiosity.
Story Hook: connect to something bigger, better or unusual.


# Three is the Magic Number (Help us remember)

Choose the most important parts of your story, then use these tricks to fix them in your audience’s memory.

The human brain prefers information in threes—little pigs, wise men, magic wishes. We can’t hold many ideas in our heads at once, which is why shopping lists were invented. What three things do you want your audience to remember?

Tip: try this card alongside [Explore] Data Detectives to present three key facts from your research.

| Technique | Example | How it works | Prompt |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Attention Three** | James Bond | “Once is happenstance. Twice is coincidence. The third time is enemy action.” A pattern of three feels deliberate—people pay attention. | What core message can you repeat three times to get our attention? |
| **Reversal Three** | Little Pigs | Straw, sticks, then brick: set up twice, reverse on the third. Builds expectation, then delivers a twist. | What are your set ups? What’s your reversal? |
| **Moderate Three** | Goldilocks | Too hot, too cold, just right: two extremes, then the middle way. | What are your two extremes? What’s your moderate way? |


# Rolls Royce Moment (Make it vivid)

“Cruising at 60mph, the loudest noise inside the Rolls Royce comes from the electric clock.”

David Ogilvy wrote this headline for a 1960 US magazine advert. It was so effective that the following year Ford spent millions claiming their cars were “as quiet as a Rolls Royce.” How can you create a similarly memorable Rolls Royce moment in your own stories?

Tip: try this tactic alongside [Explore] Data Detectives to bring research to life.

Ogilvy wrote the ticking clock headline after spending days trawling through engineers’ reports. His lesson for storytellers: do your homework. Here’s why the advert worked:

| Point | Why it works | Prompt |
|---|---|---|
| **a. It’s a vivid moment.** | In just a few words, Ogilvy gave us a rich, sensory movie we could play in our heads. | What’s the vivid moment you can describe, something we can hear, see, taste, smell or feel? |
| **b. It’s exemplary.** | This one small moment stands for the whole. It tells us that Rolls is a precision engineering company that pays attention to details. | What’s the single fact or detail that tells us everything we need to know about your product? |
| **c. It’s widely relatable.** | Even if you haven’t driven a Rolls, you know what a noisy car feels like at 60mph. This contrast makes the quality feel real. | How can you make your Rolls Royce moment relatable to something your audience will understand? |

# Leave it Out! (Create some mystery)

Tell us you’ve left something out and we will work hard to fill in the gap. And then we feel like it’s our story too.

How big is the dragon on the front of this box? Waaay big! How do you know, you can’t see him? Sometimes what we don’t see is the best part of the story. Horror movies give us a hint of danger, and we imagine the rest. Ernest Hemingway told the saddest story in just six words: “For sale. Baby shoes. Never worn.”

Tip: try [Explore] Data Detectives to find story-worthy gaps in your research.

Leave it Out!
When you’ve got a first draft of your story or presentation (or you’re trying to freshen up an old one) try this:
1. What could I leave out that my audience is expecting to hear?
2. How could I hint at what’s been left out?
3. What gaps would people try to fill for themselves?
4. How can I make them ask “What happens next?” or “How did we get here?”
5. How could I leave them wanting more?

And for a test worthy of Hemingway—how could you write your presentation in just six words?

Mind the Gap
We don’t like gaps in our knowledge, but we tend to fill them with assumptions and prejudices. So it’s worth doing a sense-check with your audience:
1. Have they filled in the gaps in the way you expected?
2. If not, what does this tell you about your assumptions and theirs?


# Show & Tell (Control our attention)

Keep people’s attention on you while you make a presentation. Make your Show and Tell work together.

Presentations fail when the speaker either describes exactly what is on the screen or speaks about something completely unrelated. The washing line method is the solution.

Tip: try [Function] Icebreaker Stories for further visual thinking.

1. Boring Structure — when the script follows the images too closely.

```
Show  ─────────────────────────────►
              Zzzz...
Tell  - - - - - - - - - - - - - - -►
```

2. Confusing Structure — when the script strays too far from the images being shown.

```
Show  ─────────────────────────────►
Tell  - - - - \
               \  Huh?
                \
                 └──────────────────►
```

3. Engaging Structure — The Washing Line
Show an image, explain it briefly, then add detail before introducing a new image and “pegging” attention back to it.

```
Show  ────●────────●────────●──────●──►
          ↑ Cool!  ↑ Interesting ↑ Wow
Tell  ────┘\______/└\______/└\____/└──►
```

Peg rhythm: touch the Show line (peg attention) → loop away with Tell (detail) → peg again on the next image.
