# Man in a Hole (Show lessons learned)

This is the story of your first day at school or your first job. It’s every fear you faced and every lesson you’ve learned.

You’re in a stable situation. Then something knocks you down—a pandemic, a job loss, a sudden setback. You have to climb out. That climb shows your strength. Use this tactic to develop a [Function] Simple Sales Story.

Kurt Vonnegut’s classic story shape has five basic beats:

```
                                              Better place ●───
                                             /
                                            /
                               Recovery ●──/
                                          /
  Comfort zone ●───────                  /
                       \                /
                        \              /
               Trigger ● \            /
                          \          /
                           \        /
                            Crisis ●
```

| Beat | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Comfort zone** | A place where nothing is necessarily wrong, but something is missing or potential is being wasted. |
| **Trigger** | An event (unlucky or due to lack of attention) that knocks the protagonist down. |
| **Crisis** | Being down in a hole. In stories, this is where one finds “treasure in the dark”—valuable lessons or items. |
| **Recovery** | Putting learned lessons to use and starting to climb back up. |
| **Better place** | Reaching a new plateau, being older, wiser, and more resilient. |

Flow: Comfort zone → Trigger → Crisis → Recovery → Better place (flat → down → bottom → up → higher flat).


# Rags to Riches (Show hidden value)

This is the story of how you turned your life around. It’s every time you showed people your true value. It’s every “you can do it!” story.

Tip: if your Rags to Riches story seems too good to be true, try [Structure] No Easy Way instead.

```
                                              Deserved Recognition ●───
                                             /
                                            /
                                 Struggle ●/
                                          /
                                         /
                              Trigger ●/
                                      /
                                     /
                     Hidden Value ●─
```

| Beat | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Hidden Value** | The hero is in a bad place physically, emotionally or socially—but something valuable inside them (or their situation) is invisible to others. Examples: Cinderella (loving child treated badly); Rocky (small-time boxer in the slums). |
| **Trigger** | Something happens that makes the hero want to change (e.g. Cinderella hears about the Ball; Rocky gets a title shot). |
| **Struggle** | The hero gets outside help at first, but ultimately they must face the challenge on their own. |
| **Deserved Recognition** | The hero’s hidden value becomes visible to everyone. Success isn’t always a traditional “win” (Rocky loses his fight, but everyone sees he is a fighter; Cinderella finds love, not just a palace). |

Flow: Hidden Value → Trigger → Struggle → Deserved Recognition (rising climb).

What is the “hidden value” that drives you onwards?
If this is your user’s story, what role do you play? (e.g. The Fairy Godmother / Guide — see [Character] Hero & Guide)


# No Easy Way (Keep it real)

Nothing good is ever easy. If something was good and easy, we’d be doing it already.

This arc shows the rise and fall of fortunes—a realistic story of struggle, setback, and earning the better place.

Tip: compare this story arc to the POPP arc in [Function] Pitch Perfect.

```
        Early Success ●                              Better Place ●───
                     / \                                        /
                    /   \                                      /
  - - - - - - - - -/ - - \ - - - Setback ● - - - - - - - - - -/ - - -  (baseline)
                  /       \         / \                       /
       Problem ●─          \       /   \                     /
                            \     /     \           Recovery ●
                             \   /       \               /
                              \ /         \             /
                                           Crisis ●─────
```

| Beat | Meaning | Example (Harry Potter) |
|---|---|---|
| **Problem** | A bad place, where danger lurks or potential is unrealized. | Harry living under the stairs. |
| **Early Success** | The hero takes an opportunity and things start to change. | Harry goes to Hogwarts. |
| **Setback** | The hero’s own weakness, or hostile reaction of others, make things turn bad. | Harry encounters Dementors, Death Eaters, and Slytherins. |
| **Crisis** | It gets so bad, maybe the hero would’ve been better off not trying to change. | No one was trying to kill Harry at the Dursleys! |
| **Recovery** | The hero learns where true strength lies. | Harry relies on friends and the power of love. |
| **Better Place** | Danger is averted, potential is realized. | Harry becomes a wizard, Voldemort is defeated. |

Flow: Problem → Early Success → Setback → Crisis → Recovery → Better Place (up / down / deeper down / up / higher).

What’s your version of No Easy Way, as innovators?
What’s the version you could tell about your user?
If it’s your user’s journey, at what point do you join them? Are you Dumbledore to your user’s Harry? (see [Character] Hero & Guide)


# Pride & Fall (Convince your skeptics)

This is one of the oldest stories we know. Icarus, Frankenstein and Titanic all follow its arc.

Pride and Fall is the favourite story for our inner sceptic. Its moral is “this sounds too good to be true”. It’s the story your clients or users might be hearing, even though you’re telling them [Structure] Rags to Riches. Use this story arc to get ahead of their scepticism and win them round.

Tip: Flip a failure story into a tale of “hard lessons learned” by using [Structure] Man in a Hole.

```
                    Pride ●
                         / \
                        /   \
                       /     \
  - - - - - - - - - - / - - - \ - - - Warning - - - - - - - - - -  (baseline)
                     /         \         (ignored)
                    /           \
         Bad Place ●             \
                                  \
                                   Fall ●
                                     \
                                      \
                                       Worse Place ●───
```

| Beat | Meaning (Icarus) |
|---|---|
| **Bad place** | Icarus is imprisoned on an island. |
| **Pride** | Daedalus builds him some wings out of wax and feathers. Icarus can defy gravity and nature. |
| **Warning** | “Don’t fly too close to the sun.” |
| **Fall** | Icarus ignores the advice, flies too close to the sun and the wings melt. |
| **Worse place** | Instead of imprisoned, the boy drowns in the sea. |

Flow: Bad place → Pride → Warning (ignored) → Fall → Worse place (up then crash lower than start).

To convince the skeptics:
- What’s their bad place: the problem you’re trying to solve?
- What might sound proud, like you’re trying to fly too high?
- Which warnings might you be ignoring?
- How might you fall?
- How could this leave your user/client worse off than they started?

See if you can reassure the sceptics with [Structure] No Easy Way.


# Downfall (Learn from failure)

Macbeth, Harvey Weinstein and Nokia: they all had a hidden flaw that brought them down.

Downfall stories are driven by Schadenfreude—joy in another’s misfortune—and they’re instructive for learning from others’ mistakes. Use this for a post-mortem session on a failed project.

You can use a Downfall story in two ways:
- Defend: You are the “mighty” one trying to hold onto your position.
- Exploit: You are the upstart wanting to take customers from a big rival.

```
  Flawed Power ●───
                  \
                   \
  - - - - - - - - - \ - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - -  (baseline)
                     \
                      \
             Desperate Denial ●
                           \
                            \
                             \
                              Deserved Disgrace ●───
```

| Beat | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Flawed Power** | A high-status position with a hidden, selfish flaw (e.g. ambition, complacency). |
| **Desperate Denial** | Flaws begin to show, but the character doubles down and covers up rather than changing. |
| **Deserved Disgrace** | Flaws are fully exposed, the character crashes down, providing relief to others. |

Flow: Flawed Power → Desperate Denial → Deserved Disgrace (high → slide → crash).

1. Defend Against Downfall
- What are your flaws and weaknesses? How might they be exposed?
- What can you learn from criticism? How might you change?
- If you do come crashing down, what benefits might there be to others? What kind of story could you tell?

2. Exploit Another’s Downfall
- What are your rival’s flaws? How can you expose them?
- How can you show your target customers that they deserve better?
- How can you show your values in a positive light by comparison?
- How would the world be a better place if all your rivals’ flaws were exposed?


# Epic Fail (Avoid the blame game)

When your plans go wrong, you can play the blame game. Or you can make sense of the failure with a story.

A study of failed startups found people tell different types of stories to explain what went wrong—some blame themselves, others a villain, and others external factors. Understanding these stories helps deal with inevitable setbacks.

Compare this technique with [Structure] Downfall.

Six basic stories (Narrative Attributions of Entrepreneurial Failure):

a. Catharsis: suffering and rebirth
You say: “that was my fault, and I’ve learned from it”
- Pros: shows humility and an open mind
- Cons: have you learned the right lessons?

b. Hubris: collective overconfidence
You say: “we tried to run before we could walk”
- Pros: puts the focus on team failings
- Cons: less emphasis on personal responsibility

c. Betrayal: blame someone close
You say: “he let me down” or “she wasn’t up to it”
- Pros: shows skills gaps in the team
- Cons: gets very personal and accusatory

d. Mechanistic: impersonal or organisational failure
You say: “our system let us down”
- Pros: puts focus on complex causes
- Cons: minimises personal failings

e. Zeitgeist: the spirit of the age
You say: “everyone was doing the same”
- Pros: puts the failure into wider context
- Cons: other people’s actions are no excuse

f. Nemesis: someone deliberately brought you down
You say: “they had it in for us”
- Pros: identifies bad relationships
- Cons: assumes bad intentions


# Voyage & Return (Focus on change)

Put adventure and the ties of home at the heart of your story. This will keep us hooked til the end.

The Odyssey and Alice in Wonderland follow this archetype: the hero enters a strange world, learns, and returns changed—which in turn makes “home” feel different.

Use this for an innovation journey (you are the hero) or a customer journey (the customer is the hero).

Home: safe, but dull.
- Why is the hero anxious for adventure?
- What do they want?

Voyage: into the strange new world.
- How does the journey begin?
- What dangers and opportunities appear?
- What pulls them back toward home?

Return: to the old world, but different.
- How has the hero changed?
- How will they make home better?

Circular journey (Order above / Chaos below; clockwise from Comfort zone):

| 1. Comfort zone | 2. Need or desire | 3. Unfamiliar situation | 4. Adaptation |
|---|---|---|---|
| 8. Having changed | 7. Return to comfort | 6. But pay a price | 5. Get what they want |

- Top row → left-to-right into the voyage (Order → Chaos)
- Bottom row ← right-to-left on the return (Chaos → Order, changed)
- Top half of the circle = Order / Home–Return; bottom half = Chaos / Voyage


# Happy Ever Afters (Craft an ending)

We’re suckers for a happy ending. Life is no fairy tale, but you can always give your story a satisfying end.

Stories illustrate growing up, finding one’s place, fitting in, and doing the right thing. Does your Hero achieve these things by the end of your story? If not, can you at least hold out the hope of a happy ending to lift our spirits?

Tip: start at The End, then show how the hero got there. Use [Structure] Five Ts for a timeline approach.

Happy Ever After = Your Hero Changes by:

a. Growing Up
Examples: Luke Skywalker (farm boy to Jedi); Harry Potter (boy to wizard).
- How has your hero grown?
- What life-stage transition have they achieved? (see [Concept] Circle of Life)
- How are they older and wiser?
- How have you helped?

b. Finding Home, Love or Respect
Examples: Odysseus returning to Penelope; Beauty taming The Beast.
- How does your hero find a place in the world? Has “home” changed after the journey? (see [Structure] Voyage & Return)
- How is your hero more worthy of love?
- How have they earned the respect of those who matter?
- How have you helped?

c. Doing the Right Thing
Examples: Ripley fighting the Alien queen; Rastamouse “making a bad thing good.”
- What’s the bad thing your hero faced? (see [Concept] Three Great Conflicts)
- How did they put the bad thing right?
- What did it cost them?
- How did you help?


# Five Ts (Shape your story)

Keep these Ts in your back pocket in case someone puts you on the spot and you need to tell a story right now!

- Timeline: beginning, middle and end.
- Turning Points: realisation, decision and change.
- Tensions: anxious moments and conflict.
- Temptations: easy way out or do the right thing?
- Teachable Moments: the moral of your story.

1. Timeline: Beginning, Middle and End.
- “At first...” [problem]
- “Then we tried...” [action]
- “And now we...” [result]

Alternative (Before and After):
- “We used to do/think...” [status quo]
- “But now...” [new order]

2. Turning Points: key moments in the timeline.
- “We realised we had a big problem when...”
- “We made a breakthrough when...”
- “We knew we had to act when...”
- “Everything changed when...”

3. Tensions: Start with the moment of maximum conflict or anxiety, then tell us how it works out. For example, “At the start of the Covid pandemic, I lost all my clients in a week...”

4. Temptations: Was there a moment when you could have been lazy, told a lie or taken the easy way out, but instead you did the right thing?

5. Teachable Moment:
- “So I guess my point is...”
- “I’ll never forget...”
- “What this taught me is...”

Which version do you feel most comfortable telling? This matters, because we can sense when you’re “putting on a show” rather than speaking “naturally.”
